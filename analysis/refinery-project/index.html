Cutting Refined Oil Imports?

M.H · 23 October 2026

Madagascar has been highly dependent on refined oil imports over the past decade. A recent article suggests that a greenfield project could reduce these imports by up to 70 per cent. While the outcome of such a project would be significant for the country, data shows that price volatility could also harm the local economy.

Exploratory policy recommendations:

  • Estimate the expected value added across sectors before committing to a national refinery partnership
  • Assess whether investing in secure, high-quality storage capacity might offer greater benefits.

According to available data, Madagascar only imports refined oil that has already undergone cracking process. Refining technology is highly capital-intensive and requires specialised expertise, which is why the technological gap is filled through imports.

In a scenario where refined imports were to be reduced by 70 per cent, the difference would have to be compensated by crude oil. Refining margins fluctuate with the business cycle, alternating between profitable and loss periods. Furthermore, crude oil prices are highly volatile, and, as illustrated in the first example, major exporters continue to refine at negative margins when market conditions are unfavourable. In years with negative margins, Madagascar would make a net loss from running a refining industry.

These findings are particularly relevant given that Madagascar is considering the construction of a costly refinery project with the support of a Canadian group. While this could reduce dependence on refined fuel imports, the country would remain vulnerable to crude price volatility and negative refining margins in a highly concentrated industry.
